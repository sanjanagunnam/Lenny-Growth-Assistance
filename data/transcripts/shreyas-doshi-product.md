# Guest: Shreyas Doshi
# Episode: High-Agency Product Leadership, Ruthless Prioritization, and the LNO Framework
# Source: Lenny's Podcast

**Lenny Rachitsky:** Welcome to Lenny's Podcast. My guest today is Shreyas Doshi. Shreyas has been a product leader at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo, and is widely regarded as one of the sharpest thinkers and mentors on product management and career progression. In today's episode, we explore the psychology of high-agency operators, why prioritization fails at most fast-growing companies, and Shreyas's famous frameworks including the LNO framework and High-Return On Investment product thinking. Shreyas, welcome to the show.

**Shreyas Doshi:** Thank you Lenny, thrilled to be here.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** One of your concepts that resonated most across tech is the LNO Framework for personal leverage and time management. Could you break down what LNO stands for and how leaders can apply it immediately?

**Shreyas Doshi:** The LNO framework is designed to cure a major affliction among high achievers: the perfectionist trap. When talented people join a company, they got there by being thorough, meticulous, and doing great work on everything. But as your scope grows, giving 100% effort to 100% of tasks leads to burnout, delayed deliverables, and poor strategic judgment.

LNO classifies your daily and weekly work into three distinct buckets:
1. **L: Leverage Tasks (10x Return)**: These are the critical tasks where an extraordinary outcome dramatically shifts the company's trajectory. Examples include designing a core product architecture, hiring an exceptional staff engineer, establishing a partnership agreement, or setting quarterly strategy. On Leverage tasks, you must strive for perfection and obsess over excellence.
2. **N: Neutral Tasks (1x Return)**: These are the standard operational necessities. Status reports, attending cross-functional check-ins, answering standard customer emails, and routine sprint planning. On Neutral tasks, doing an A+ job yields zero additional business return compared to doing a B+ job. You should aim for "good enough" and move quickly.
3. **O: Overhead Tasks (<1x Return)**: These are low-value operational chores that consume cognitive bandwidth—logging tickets, administrative forms, unnecessary multi-stakeholder approval loops. On Overhead tasks, you should actively seek to automate, delegate, batch, or execute with minimal acceptable effort (a C-grade execution that meets requirements).

Most struggling product managers spend 60% of their day giving A+ effort to Neutral and Overhead tasks, leaving them exhausted with only crumbs of attention for Leverage tasks. High-agency leaders aggressively protect their mornings for Leverage work.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** You mentioned "High Agency." That term has become very popular lately, but people often confuse it with being stubborn, aggressive, or dismissive of process. What does true high agency look like in practice?

**Shreyas Doshi:** High agency is the refusal to accept the default world. When an average person encounters a roadblock—say, "Legal said we can't launch in Germany for six months" or "The data pipeline is broken so we can't measure activation"—an average person stops and says, "Well, my hands are tied, I sent an email to Legal and now I'm waiting."

A high-agency person bends reality to their will through curiosity, empathy, and relentless resourcefulness. They don't fight Legal; they walk over to the lawyer's desk and say: "Help me understand the exact regulatory risk. Is it GDPR data residency? If we pseudonymize the user tokens in transit, does that resolve the legal liability?" Nine times out of ten, the blocker isn't an immovable law of physics; it's a communication gap or an outdated policy.

High-agency people find a way when there seems to be no way. They don't complain about external constraints; they treat constraints as creative boundaries to engineer a breakthrough.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** At Stripe, you worked on building products that processed billions of dollars. How did Stripe maintain high quality while moving at breakneck speed?

**Shreyas Doshi:** At Stripe, there was an uncompromising culture of craft and written clarity. We rarely had meetings where someone presented slides. Instead, someone wrote a two-to-six-page document detailing the customer problem, the proposed solution, the API contract, and the counter-arguments.

Writing forces clarity of thought. You cannot hide intellectual laziness behind charismatic storytelling in a memo. If your logic has a hole, it is obvious in paragraph three.

Another key mechanism was what Patrick and John Collison modeled: extreme attention to the user's friction. At Stripe, if an API error message was ambiguous or returned an unclear HTTP status code, that was treated as a bug with the same severity as a minor outage. That reverence for developer ergonomics is why developers fell in love with Stripe.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** If you could leave product managers and founders with one mental model for career longevity and impact, what would it be?

**Shreyas Doshi:** Optimize for learning velocity over title velocity. Early in your career, people chase Senior PM, Group PM, Director titles. But titles are arbitrary and depend heavily on corporate politics. What compounds exponentially is your taste, your judgment under uncertainty, and your ability to build high-trust relationships with extraordinary engineers and designers. If you become someone who consistently turns ambiguous customer problems into elegant, profitable software, you will never lack opportunity.
