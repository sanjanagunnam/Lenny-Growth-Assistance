# Guest: Brian Chesky
# Episode: Leading Through Crisis, Designing for Love, and the Return to Founder Mode
# Source: Lenny's Podcast

**Lenny Rachitsky:** Welcome to Lenny's Podcast. Today, my guest is Brian Chesky, co-founder and CEO of Airbnb. Brian led Airbnb from three air mattresses on the floor of a San Francisco apartment to a global brand facilitating over 1.5 billion guest arrivals. In this conversation, we talk about how Airbnb rebuilt its entire product engine, eliminated traditional product management in favor of product marketing and design-led development, and how founders must stay in the details rather than abdicating responsibility to organizational bureaucracy. Brian, thank you so much for being here.

**Brian Chesky:** Thanks Lenny, great to be with you. It's always fun to talk to people who really care about craft and building.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** Let's dive right into the reorganization you announced that sparked so much discussion in Silicon Valley: getting rid of the traditional product management function. What really happened, and why did you decide to make that radical shift?

**Brian Chesky:** First, let me clarify what we actually did because a lot of people misread the headlines. We did not fire all our PMs. What we did was combine product management with product marketing, and we anchored product leadership in design and engineering. Before the pandemic, Airbnb had become bloated. We had hundreds of teams running in dozens of different directions. Every team had an autonomous roadmap. One team was optimizing the checkout button, another was building a separate search experience, and nobody was looking at the end-to-end customer journey.

When COVID hit in March 2020, our business dropped by 80% in eight weeks. We were staring into the abyss. We had to lay off 25% of our staff, cancel all non-core projects, and get back to the essence of what Airbnb is: hosts opening their homes to travelers. During that existential crisis, I realized that delegating everything to decentralized product managers had broken the company's cohesion. The product felt like a quilt sewn together by twenty different people who didn't talk to each other.

So I decided to run the company like an orchestra. In an orchestra, you have first violins, cellos, percussion, woodwinds. They don't each play their own song. There is one conductor, one score, and everyone plays in harmony. We created a single company roadmap. Every six months, we do a release. Every single feature that ships goes through a rigorous design review with me and our head of design.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** That concept of the CEO being in the details has since become known as "Founder Mode." For leaders who are trying to practice this without micromanaging in a toxic way, where is the line between healthy detail obsession and destructive meddling?

**Brian Chesky:** The conventional Silicon Valley wisdom for twenty years has been: hire great people and get out of their way. But that advice was largely created by professional managers who didn't know how to build things. If you abdicate your product vision to people five layers down in the organization, you don't get empowerment; you get chaos. You get people optimizing for local maximums instead of the global mission.

The difference between micromanagement and deep involvement is trust and coaching. When I review a product detail, I'm not just telling the team "change this button color." I am teaching them how to see the customer experience. I ask: "What does the guest feel when they land on this page? Why are there four fonts on this screen? If this were an Apple product, what would Steve Jobs say about this transition?"

True leadership is about pushing people to achieve what they didn't believe they were capable of. When we rebuilt our host onboarding flow, the initial version took 40 clicks. I told the team to make it 10 clicks. They said it was impossible because of regulatory requirements, photo uploads, and identity verification. We sat in a room for three days, dismantled every assumption, and ended up launching Airbnb Setup with just a few intuitive steps. The team was proud because we made something legendary.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** How do you think about growth loops versus paid acquisition? Airbnb famously cut almost all performance marketing during 2020 and saw traffic rebound to 95% of previous levels purely through direct and PR traffic. What did that teach you about defensible growth?

**Brian Chesky:** It taught us that performance marketing is like an addictive drug. If you buy growth on Google or Facebook, the moment you stop paying the toll, the traffic evaporates. Before the pandemic, we were spending hundreds of millions of dollars a year bidding on our own brand name on search engines. It was insanity.

When we turned off performance marketing, our organic traffic barely budged. Why? Because Airbnb is a noun and a verb. People don't go to Google and search "temporary lodging in Paris"; they go directly to Airbnb or search "Airbnb Paris". That brand equity was built through PR, storytelling, and an unforgettable product experience.

Our real growth loop is the host-guest loop:
1. A guest has a magical trip in an Airbnb.
2. They realize their host is earning real income by renting an extra room or vacation house.
3. The guest returns home and decides to list their own property.
4. More listings increase geographic coverage and unique inventory.
5. Unique inventory attracts more guests at lower customer acquisition cost.

If you don't have a product-driven growth loop, you don't have a business; you have a customer acquisition arbitrage scheme.

**Lenny Rachitsky:** What advice would you give to early-stage founders who are trying to find product-market fit today in an AI-dominated landscape?

**Brian Chesky:** Do things that don't scale. Paul Graham told us that in Y Combinator in 2009, and it remains the single best piece of advice ever given to entrepreneurs. Joe Gebbia and I literally went door-to-door in New York City with a camera, knocking on our hosts' doors, taking professional photos of their apartments, and writing down their complaints by hand. We lived with our users.

Today, everyone wants to launch an AI app, look at a Mixpanel dashboard, run an A/B test with 50 visitors, and pretend they understand human psychology. You cannot understand people through an analytics dashboard. You have to look into their eyes, feel their confusion, and design something so good that they tell ten of their closest friends. If you can make 100 people fall in love with your product, you can conquer the world. Making a million people merely like you is useless.
